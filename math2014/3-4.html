SECTION 3.4

Inner Product & Cross Product

We can already add vectors and scale them. Now we ask: can we multiply two vectors, and get something useful? There are two famous answers, and they're wonderfully different in flavour. The dot product eats two vectors and spits out a single number — it measures length and angle. The cross product (only in 3-D) eats two vectors and returns a new vector perpendicular to both — it measures area and gives directions. Between them they power nearly all of the geometry in this course.

1 · The dot product: multiply, then add

Definition · dot (inner, scalar) product

For $\vec a=[a_1,a_2,\dots,a_n]$ and $\vec b=[b_1,b_2,\dots,b_n]$ (same size), the dot product is the number

$$\vec a\cdot\vec b=a_1b_1+a_2b_2+\cdots+a_nb_n.$$

The result is a scalar (a single real number), not a vector — which is why it's also called the scalar product or inner product, written $\langle\vec a,\vec b\rangle$.

Intuition · "line them up, multiply matching entries, total it"

It's the same "multiply corresponding things and add" move you used inside matrix multiplication (every entry of $AB$ was a dot product of a row with a column). Here it stands alone. The payoff, coming in part 4, is that this plain arithmetic secretly encodes the angle between the two vectors.

Example 1 · a dot product in $\R^5$

$\vec w=[2,4,-3,2,8]$, $\vec x=[3,1,0,-1,-2]$. Multiply matching entries and sum:

$$\vec w\cdot\vec x=2(3)+4(1)+(-3)(0)+2(-1)+8(-2)=6+4+0-2-16=-8.$$

One number out. (The negative sign will mean something in part 5: these two vectors point in generally opposite directions.)

2 · The norm: a vector's length

Definition · norm (length/magnitude)

The norm $\lVert\vec u\rVert$ of a vector is the square root of the dot product of the vector with itself:

$$\lVert\vec u\rVert=\sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2+\cdots+u_n^2}=\sqrt{\vec u\cdot\vec u}.$$

Intuition · it's just Pythagoras, in any dimension

In the plane, the length of the arrow to $(u_1,u_2)$ is $\sqrt{u_1^2+u_2^2}$ by the Pythagorean theorem. The norm simply extends that to $n$ entries: square every component, add, square-root. The key identity $\lVert\vec u\rVert^2=\vec u\cdot\vec u$ links length and dot product — it's the bridge we'll cross constantly.

Example 2 · norms of two vectors

For $\vec w=[2,4,-3,2,8]$ and $\vec x=[3,1,0,-1,-2]$:

$$\lVert\vec w\rVert=\sqrt{2^2+4^2+(-3)^2+2^2+8^2}=\sqrt{4+16+9+4+64}=\sqrt{97},$$ $$\lVert\vec x\rVert=\sqrt{3^2+1^2+0^2+(-1)^2+(-2)^2}=\sqrt{9+1+0+1+4}=\sqrt{15}.$$

Properties of the dot product

For vectors $\vec a,\vec b,\vec c$ and scalar $c$: (1) $\vec a\cdot\vec a=\lVert\vec a\rVert^2$; (2) $\vec a\cdot\vec b=\vec b\cdot\vec a$ (symmetric); (3) $\vec a\cdot(\vec b+\vec c)=\vec a\cdot\vec b+\vec a\cdot\vec c$ (distributes); (4) $(c\vec a)\cdot\vec b=c(\vec a\cdot\vec b)=\vec a\cdot(c\vec b)$; (5) $\mathbf 0\cdot\vec a=0$.

3 · The dot product knows the angle

Here's the beautiful fact that makes the dot product so useful: it equals the product of the two lengths times the cosine of the angle between the vectors.

Geometric formula

If $\theta$ is the angle between nonzero vectors $\vec a$ and $\vec b$ (with $0\le\theta\le\pi$), then

$$\boxed{\;\vec a\cdot\vec b=\lVert\vec a\rVert\,\lVert\vec b\rVert\cos\theta.\;}$$

Rearranged, this finds the angle: $\displaystyle\cos\theta=\frac{\vec a\cdot\vec b}{\lVert\vec a\rVert\,\lVert\vec b\rVert}$.

Why it's true · the Law of Cosines in disguise

Place $\vec a$ and $\vec b$ as arrows from the origin, reaching points $A$ and $B$; the third side of the triangle is $\vec a-\vec b$. The Law of Cosines on triangle $OAB$ says $\lVert\vec a-\vec b\rVert^2=\lVert\vec a\rVert^2+\lVert\vec b\rVert^2-2\lVert\vec a\rVert\lVert\vec b\rVert\cos\theta$. But expand the left side with dot-product algebra: $\lVert\vec a-\vec b\rVert^2=(\vec a-\vec b)\cdot(\vec a-\vec b)=\lVert\vec a\rVert^2-2\,\vec a\cdot\vec b+\lVert\vec b\rVert^2$. Compare the two: the $\lVert\vec a\rVert^2$ and $\lVert\vec b\rVert^2$ cancel, leaving $-2\,\vec a\cdot\vec b=-2\lVert\vec a\rVert\lVert\vec b\rVert\cos\theta$, i.e. $\vec a\cdot\vec b=\lVert\vec a\rVert\lVert\vec b\rVert\cos\theta$. $\blacksquare$

Example 3 · dot product from lengths and angle

If $\lVert\vec a\rVert=4$, $\lVert\vec b\rVert=6$, and the angle between them is $\theta=\pi/3$ (so $\cos\theta=\tfrac12$), then

$$\vec a\cdot\vec b=\lVert\vec a\rVert\,\lVert\vec b\rVert\cos\theta=4\cdot6\cdot\tfrac12=12.$$

Example 4 · the angle between two vectors

Find the angle between $\vec a=[2,2,-1]$ and $\vec b=[5,-3,2]$. Compute the three ingredients:

$$\vec a\cdot\vec b=2(5)+2(-3)+(-1)(2)=10-6-2=2,\quad \lVert\vec a\rVert=\sqrt{4+4+1}=3,\quad \lVert\vec b\rVert=\sqrt{25+9+4}=\sqrt{38}.$$ $$\cos\theta=\frac{\vec a\cdot\vec b}{\lVert\vec a\rVert\lVert\vec b\rVert}=\frac{2}{3\sqrt{38}}\quad\Rightarrow\quad \theta=\arccos\frac{2}{3\sqrt{38}}\approx84.8^\circ.$$

4 · Orthogonality: the dot product's party trick

Definition · orthogonal (perpendicular) vectors

Two nonzero vectors are orthogonal (perpendicular) if the angle between them is $\theta=\pi/2$. Since $\cos(\pi/2)=0$, the formula collapses to the cleanest test in the whole chapter:

$$\vec a\perp\vec b\iff \vec a\cdot\vec b=0.$$

Intuition · the sign of the dot product tells you the general direction

Because $\vec a\cdot\vec b=\lVert\vec a\rVert\lVert\vec b\rVert\cos\theta$ and the lengths are positive, the sign of $\vec a\cdot\vec b$ is the sign of $\cos\theta$:

  • $\vec a\cdot\vec b>0$: angle is acute, the vectors point in roughly the same direction.
  • $\vec a\cdot\vec b=0$: angle is exactly $90^\circ$, perpendicular.
  • $\vec a\cdot\vec b<0$: angle is obtuse, they point in roughly opposite directions.

(That's why Example 1's $-8$ meant "generally opposing.") No need to compute the actual angle to read off this qualitative relationship.

Example 5 · a perpendicularity check

Show $[2,2,-1]\perp[5,-4,2]$. Just dot them: $\;[2,2,-1]\cdot[5,-4,2]=2(5)+2(-4)+(-1)(2)=10-8-2=0$. The dot product is $0$, so the vectors are perpendicular. (No angle computation needed — zero is the certificate.)

5 · The cross product: a vector perpendicular to two others

The cross product is special to three dimensions. Given two vectors in $\R^3$, it produces a third vector that is perpendicular to both — exactly what you need to find a normal direction to a plane, or the area of a parallelogram.

Definition · cross (vector) product

For $\vec x=(x_1,x_2,x_3)$ and $\vec y=(y_1,y_2,y_3)$ in $\R^3$,

$$\vec x\times\vec y=(x_2y_3-x_3y_2,\;\; x_3y_1-x_1y_3,\;\; x_1y_2-x_2y_1).$$

The result is a vector (hence "vector product"), and it's defined only for 3-D vectors.

Tip · the determinant mnemonic (how everyone actually computes it)

Nobody memorises those three formulas. Instead, write a "formal determinant" with the unit vectors $\hat i,\hat j,\hat k$ on top and expand along row 1:

$$\vec x\times\vec y=\det\begin{bmatrix}\hat i&\hat j&\hat k\\ x_1&x_2&x_3\\ y_1&y_2&y_3\end{bmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}x_2&x_3\\y_2&y_3\end{vmatrix}\hat i-\begin{vmatrix}x_1&x_3\\y_1&y_3\end{vmatrix}\hat j+\begin{vmatrix}x_1&x_2\\y_1&y_2\end{vmatrix}\hat k.$$

Watch the minus sign on the middle ($\hat j$) term — it's the single most common cross-product mistake.

Example 6 · a cross product by the determinant trick

For $\vec a=(1,3,4)$ and $\vec b=(2,7,-5)$:

$$\vec a\times\vec b=\begin{vmatrix}\hat i&\hat j&\hat k\\1&3&4\\2&7&-5\end{vmatrix}=\begin{vmatrix}3&4\\7&-5\end{vmatrix}\hat i-\begin{vmatrix}1&4\\2&-5\end{vmatrix}\hat j+\begin{vmatrix}1&3\\2&7\end{vmatrix}\hat k.$$

Each $2\times2$ via $ad-bc$: $\;(3\cdot-5-4\cdot7)=-43$; $\;(1\cdot-5-4\cdot2)=-13$; $\;(1\cdot7-3\cdot2)=1$. With the middle sign flip:

$$\vec a\times\vec b=-43\hat i-(-13)\hat j+1\hat k=-43\hat i+13\hat j+\hat k=(-43,13,1).$$

Example 7 · a vector crossed with itself is zero

For any $\vec a=(a_1,a_2,a_3)$, the determinant for $\vec a\times\vec a$ has two identical rows, so it's $\mathbf 0$. Directly: each component like $a_2a_3-a_3a_2=0$. Hence $\vec a\times\vec a=\mathbf 0$. (Makes sense: a vector can't be perpendicular to itself, and the "parallelogram" it spans with itself is flat.)

6 · What the cross product means geometrically

Theorem · $\vec a\times\vec b$ is perpendicular to both $\vec a$ and $\vec b$

The vector $\vec a\times\vec b$ is orthogonal to $\vec a$ and to $\vec b$ (so it's perpendicular to the whole plane they span).

Example 8 · proof that $(\vec a\times\vec b)\cdot\vec a=0$

Dot $\vec a\times\vec b$ with $\vec a=(a_1,a_2,a_3)$:

$$(\vec a\times\vec b)\cdot\vec a=a_1(a_2b_3-a_3b_2)-a_2(a_1b_3-a_3b_1)+a_3(a_1b_2-a_2b_1).$$

Multiply out: $\;a_1a_2b_3-a_1a_3b_2-a_2a_1b_3+a_2a_3b_1+a_3a_1b_2-a_3a_2b_1$. Every term cancels with another ($a_1a_2b_3$ with $-a_2a_1b_3$, etc.), giving $0$. The same computation shows $(\vec a\times\vec b)\cdot\vec b=0$. So $\vec a\times\vec b\perp\vec a$ and $\perp\vec b$. $\blacksquare$

Intuition · direction by the right-hand rule, length by $\sin\theta$

Perpendicularity leaves two choices of direction (up or down); the right-hand rule picks one: point your right hand's fingers along $\vec a$, curl them toward $\vec b$, and your thumb points along $\vec a\times\vec b$. For the length:

$$\lVert\vec a\times\vec b\rVert=\lVert\vec a\rVert\,\lVert\vec b\rVert\sin\theta,$$

which is exactly the area of the parallelogram spanned by $\vec a$ and $\vec b$ (base times height, height $=\lVert\vec b\rVert\sin\theta$). Compare the dot product's $\cos\theta$: dot uses the aligned part, cross uses the perpendicular part.

Why $\lVert\vec a\times\vec b\rVert=\lVert\vec a\rVert\lVert\vec b\rVert\sin\theta$ (sketch)

Expanding $\lVert\vec a\times\vec b\rVert^2$ and regrouping gives the identity $\lVert\vec a\times\vec b\rVert^2=\lVert\vec a\rVert^2\lVert\vec b\rVert^2-(\vec a\cdot\vec b)^2$. Substitute $\vec a\cdot\vec b=\lVert\vec a\rVert\lVert\vec b\rVert\cos\theta$: the right side becomes $\lVert\vec a\rVert^2\lVert\vec b\rVert^2(1-\cos^2\theta)=\lVert\vec a\rVert^2\lVert\vec b\rVert^2\sin^2\theta$. Take square roots (and $\sin\theta\ge0$ on $[0,\pi]$). $\blacksquare$

Parallel test

Two nonzero vectors are parallel $\iff\vec a\times\vec b=\mathbf 0$. (Parallel means $\theta=0$ or $\pi$, so $\sin\theta=0$, so the cross product has zero length.) Neat contrast: dot product $=0$ detects perpendicular; cross product $=\mathbf 0$ detects parallel.

7 · Two signature applications: normals and areas

Example 9 · a vector perpendicular to a plane through three points

Find a vector perpendicular to the plane through $P(1,4,6)$, $Q(-2,5,-1)$, $R(1,-1,1)$.

Idea: build two vectors lying in the plane, then cross them — the result is perpendicular to both, hence to the plane. Use edges from $P$:

$$\overrightarrow{PQ}=Q-P=(-3,1,-7),\qquad \overrightarrow{PR}=R-P=(0,-5,-5).$$ $$\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR}=\begin{vmatrix}\hat i&\hat j&\hat k\\-3&1&-7\\0&-5&-5\end{vmatrix}=(-5-35)\hat i-(15-0)\hat j+(15-0)\hat k=(-40,-15,15).$$

So $(-40,-15,15)$ is perpendicular to the plane — and so is any scalar multiple, e.g. the tidier $(8,3,-3)$ (divide by $-5$).

Example 10 · area of a triangle in 3-D

Find the area of triangle $PQR$ with the same $P,Q,R$. The parallelogram on $\overrightarrow{PQ},\overrightarrow{PR}$ has area $\lVert\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR}\rVert$; a triangle is half of it. From Example 9, $\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR}=(-40,-15,15)$, so

$$\lVert\overrightarrow{PQ}\times\overrightarrow{PR}\rVert=\sqrt{(-40)^2+(-15)^2+15^2}=\sqrt{1600+225+225}=\sqrt{2050}=5\sqrt{82}.$$ $$\text{Area of }\triangle PQR=\tfrac12\cdot5\sqrt{82}=\frac{5}{2}\sqrt{82}.$$

8 · Algebra of the cross product (and the scalar triple product)

Properties of the cross product

For vectors $\vec a,\vec b,\vec c$ and scalar $c$:

  1. $\vec a\times\vec b=-(\vec b\times\vec a)$  (anti-commutative — order flips the sign!)
  2. $(c\vec a)\times\vec b=c(\vec a\times\vec b)=\vec a\times(c\vec b)$
  3. $\vec a\times(\vec b+\vec c)=\vec a\times\vec b+\vec a\times\vec c$
  4. $(\vec a+\vec b)\times\vec c=\vec a\times\vec c+\vec b\times\vec c$
  5. $\vec a\cdot(\vec b\times\vec c)=(\vec a\times\vec b)\cdot\vec c$  (the scalar triple product)
  6. $\vec a\times(\vec b\times\vec c)=(\vec a\cdot\vec c)\vec b-(\vec a\cdot\vec b)\vec c$  ("BAC−CAB")

Trap · the cross product is anti-commutative, not commutative

Property 1 says swapping the order negates the result: $\vec b\times\vec a=-(\vec a\times\vec b)$. This fits the right-hand rule (flip the order, your thumb points the opposite way). Don't ever assume $\vec a\times\vec b=\vec b\times\vec a$. Also note the cross product is not associative in general: $\vec a\times(\vec b\times\vec c)\ne(\vec a\times\vec b)\times\vec c$.

The scalar triple product is a determinant (Property 5 proof)

Writing $\vec a\cdot(\vec b\times\vec c)$ out and regrouping gives exactly the $3\times3$ determinant with the three vectors as rows:

$$\vec a\cdot(\vec b\times\vec c)=\det\begin{bmatrix}a_1&a_2&a_3\\b_1&b_2&b_3\\c_1&c_2&c_3\end{bmatrix}.$$

Regrouping the same expression the other way shows it also equals $(\vec a\times\vec b)\cdot\vec c$, proving Property 5. Geometrically its absolute value is the volume of the parallelepiped spanned by $\vec a,\vec b,\vec c$ — and it's $0$ exactly when the three vectors are coplanar (linearly dependent). A lovely closing link: the determinant from §3.3 measures 3-D volume.

9 · Section recap

Dot vs cross, side by side

  • Dot product $\vec a\cdot\vec b=\sum a_ib_i$ → a number; equals $\lVert\vec a\rVert\lVert\vec b\rVert\cos\theta$; detects perpendicular ($=0$); sign gives same/opposite direction; gives length via $\lVert\vec u\rVert=\sqrt{\vec u\cdot\vec u}$ and angle via $\cos\theta=\dfrac{\vec a\cdot\vec b}{\lVert\vec a\rVert\lVert\vec b\rVert}$.
  • Cross product (3-D only) $\vec a\times\vec b$ → a vector perpendicular to both; compute by the $\hat i\hat j\hat k$ determinant (mind the middle minus); length $\lVert\vec a\rVert\lVert\vec b\rVert\sin\theta=$ parallelogram area; detects parallel ($=\mathbf 0$); anti-commutative.
  • Uses: normal to a plane = cross of two in-plane edges; triangle area = ½‖cross‖; volume / coplanarity = scalar triple product = determinant.
