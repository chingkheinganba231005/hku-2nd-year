SECTION 3.5

The Eigenvalue Problem

Multiplying a vector by a matrix usually rotates and stretches it into a new direction. But for most matrices there are a few special directions that the matrix leaves pointing the same way — it only stretches or shrinks them. Those magic directions are eigenvectors, and the stretch factors are eigenvalues. They are the deepest idea in the chapter and the key to understanding what a matrix "really does", plus a slick way to compute huge powers $A^k$.

1 · What eigenvectors and eigenvalues are

Definition · eigenvector & eigenvalue

For a square $n\times n$ matrix $A$, a nonzero vector $\vec x$ is an eigenvector of $A$ if

$$A\vec x=\lambda\vec x$$

for some scalar $\lambda$. That scalar $\lambda$ is the eigenvalue attached to $\vec x$; we say $\vec x$ is an eigenvector corresponding to $\lambda$. (The zero vector is excluded — $A\mathbf 0=\lambda\mathbf 0$ holds for every $\lambda$ and would tell us nothing.)

Intuition · "the matrix doesn't turn this vector, it just scales it"

$A\vec x=\lambda\vec x$ says: feeding $\vec x$ through $A$ gives back the same direction, merely multiplied by $\lambda$. If $\lambda=3$, $A$ triples $\vec x$; if $\lambda=-1$, $A$ flips it; if $\lambda=0$, $A$ crushes it to $\mathbf 0$ (so $\vec x$ is a nonzero solution of $A\vec x=\mathbf 0$). These invariant directions are the "grain" of the matrix — the axes along which its action is simplest.

Example 1 · verifying an eigenvector

Is $\vec v=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}$ an eigenvector of $A=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\8&-1\end{bmatrix}$? Just multiply and see if the output is a multiple of $\vec v$:

$$A\vec v=\begin{bmatrix}3&0\\8&-1\end{bmatrix}\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3\\6\end{bmatrix}=3\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\end{bmatrix}=3\vec v.$$

Yes — the output $(3,6)$ is exactly $3$ times the input, so $\vec v$ is an eigenvector with eigenvalue $\lambda=3$.

2 · Finding eigenvalues: the characteristic equation

Verifying is easy; finding eigenvalues from scratch needs one clever rearrangement. Move everything to one side: $A\vec x=\lambda\vec x$ becomes $A\vec x-\lambda\vec x=\mathbf 0$, i.e. $(A-\lambda I)\vec x=\mathbf 0$ (we insert $I$ so the "$\lambda$" becomes a matrix we can subtract).

Definition · characteristic equation

$\lambda$ is an eigenvalue of $A$ $\iff$ the homogeneous system $(A-\lambda I)\vec x=\mathbf 0$ has a nontrivial solution $\iff$ the matrix $A-\lambda I$ is singular $\iff$

$$\boxed{\;\det(A-\lambda I)=0.\;}$$

This is the characteristic equation. Its left side is a degree-$n$ polynomial in $\lambda$ (the characteristic polynomial); its roots are precisely the eigenvalues.

Intuition · why the determinant must vanish

We need a nonzero $\vec x$ with $(A-\lambda I)\vec x=\mathbf 0$. A homogeneous system has nonzero solutions exactly when its matrix is singular (§3.3), and singular exactly when its determinant is $0$. So eigenvalues are the special $\lambda$'s that make $A-\lambda I$ "collapse" (lose invertibility). Find those $\lambda$'s first, then for each one solve $(A-\lambda I)\vec x=\mathbf 0$ to get its eigenvectors.

The recipe

  1. Form $A-\lambda I$ (subtract $\lambda$ from each diagonal entry).
  2. Set $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$ and solve the polynomial for $\lambda$ — these are the eigenvalues.
  3. For each eigenvalue $\lambda$, solve the homogeneous system $(A-\lambda I)\vec x=\mathbf 0$; its nonzero solutions are the eigenvectors (a free variable always appears, since the matrix is singular).

3 · Worked examples: $2\times2$

Example 2 · full eigen-analysis of a $2\times2$

Find eigenvalues and eigenvectors of $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&6\\5&2\end{bmatrix}$.

Eigenvalues. $\det(A-\lambda I)=\det\begin{bmatrix}1-\lambda&6\\5&2-\lambda\end{bmatrix}=(1-\lambda)(2-\lambda)-30=\lambda^2-3\lambda-28=0.$ Factor: $(\lambda-7)(\lambda+4)=0$, so $\lambda=7$ or $\lambda=-4$.

Eigenvector for $\lambda_1=7$: solve $(A-7I)\vec v=\mathbf 0$, i.e. $\begin{bmatrix}-6&6\\5&-5\end{bmatrix}\vec v=\mathbf 0$. Both rows say $v_1=v_2$, so pick $\vec v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}$.

Eigenvector for $\lambda_2=-4$: solve $(A+4I)\vec v=\mathbf 0$, i.e. $\begin{bmatrix}5&6\\5&6\end{bmatrix}\vec v=\mathbf 0$, giving $5v_1+6v_2=0$, so $\vec v_2=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-5/6\end{bmatrix}$ (or the cleaner $\begin{bmatrix}6\\-5\end{bmatrix}$).

So $\lambda_1=7\leftrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\end{bmatrix}$ and $\lambda_2=-4\leftrightarrow\begin{bmatrix}6\\-5\end{bmatrix}$.

Tip · eigenvectors come in families — any nonzero multiple works

Because $(A-\lambda I)\vec x=\mathbf 0$ is homogeneous, if $\vec v$ is an eigenvector then so is every nonzero scalar multiple of $\vec v$ (they all point along the same special direction). So $\begin{bmatrix}6\\-5\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}1\\-5/6\end{bmatrix}$ are the "same" eigenvector. Just report one convenient representative, ideally with whole-number entries.

Example 3 · another $2\times2$

For $A=\begin{bmatrix}5&4\\1&2\end{bmatrix}$: $\det(A-\lambda I)=(5-\lambda)(2-\lambda)-4=\lambda^2-7\lambda+6=0=(\lambda-6)(\lambda-1)$, so $\lambda=6$ or $1$. For $\lambda=6$: $(A-6I)\vec v=\mathbf 0$ gives $\vec v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1/4\end{bmatrix}$ (i.e. $\begin{bmatrix}4\\1\end{bmatrix}$). For $\lambda=1$: $(A-I)\vec v=\mathbf 0$ gives $\vec v_2=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\end{bmatrix}$.

4 · Worked examples: $3\times3$ (and a shortcut)

Example 4 · four $3\times3$ matrices

Matrix $A=\begin{bmatrix}0&1&0\\0&0&1\\6&-11&6\end{bmatrix}$ (a companion matrix). Expanding $\det(A-\lambda I)$ gives $-\lambda^3+6\lambda^2-11\lambda+6=-(\lambda-1)(\lambda-2)(\lambda-3)$, so $\lambda=1,2,3$. Solving each $(A-\lambda I)\vec v=\mathbf 0$:

$$\lambda=1:\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix},\qquad \lambda=2:\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\4\end{bmatrix},\qquad \lambda=3:\begin{bmatrix}1\\3\\9\end{bmatrix}.$$

Three distinct eigenvalues, three independent eigenvectors — the ideal case.

Matrix $B=\begin{bmatrix}3&-2&0\\-2&3&0\\0&0&5\end{bmatrix}$ (symmetric). $\det(B-\lambda I)=(5-\lambda)^2(1-\lambda)$, so $\lambda_1=1$ and $\lambda_2=\lambda_3=5$ (a repeated eigenvalue). For $\lambda=1$: $\vec v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}$. For $\lambda=5$ the system has two free variables, giving two independent eigenvectors $\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\\0\end{bmatrix}$ and $\begin{bmatrix}0\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}$ — so even the repeated eigenvalue supplies its full quota.

Matrix $C=\begin{bmatrix}1&1&-1\\-1&3&-1\\-1&2&0\end{bmatrix}$. $\det(C-\lambda I)=-(1-\lambda)^2(2-\lambda)$, so $\lambda_1=2$, $\lambda_2=\lambda_3=1$. For $\lambda=2$: $\vec v_1=\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}$. For the repeated $\lambda=1$ here, the system has only one free variable, yielding a single direction $\begin{bmatrix}1\\1\\1\end{bmatrix}$ — a warning sign we'll return to (this matrix is "defective").

Matrix $D=\begin{bmatrix}-1&2&2\\2&2&2\\-3&-6&-6\end{bmatrix}$. $\det(D-\lambda I)=-\lambda(\lambda+2)(\lambda+3)$, so $\lambda=0,-2,-3$ (note $\lambda=0$ is allowed — it signals $D$ is singular), with eigenvectors $\begin{bmatrix}0\\1\\-1\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1/2\\0\end{bmatrix},\begin{bmatrix}1\\0\\-1\end{bmatrix}$.

Tip · a triangular matrix wears its eigenvalues on its diagonal

Theorem: the eigenvalues of a triangular (or diagonal) matrix are exactly its main-diagonal entries. Reason: $A-\lambda I$ is still triangular, so $\det(A-\lambda I)=(a_{11}-\lambda)(a_{22}-\lambda)\cdots(a_{nn}-\lambda)$, whose roots are the $a_{ii}$. So if you spot a triangular matrix, read the eigenvalues straight off the diagonal — no characteristic polynomial needed.

5 · Diagonalization: the eigen-payoff

If an $n\times n$ matrix has enough independent eigenvectors, we can rewrite it as $A=PDP^{-1}$ with $D$ diagonal. This "diagonalization" turns hard matrix questions (especially powers) into trivial ones.

Definition · diagonalizable; how to build $P$ and $D$

$A$ is diagonalizable if $A=PDP^{-1}$ for some invertible $P$ and diagonal $D$. The construction: put $n$ independent eigenvectors as the columns of $P$, and put their eigenvalues (in the matching order) on the diagonal of $D$:

$$P=[\,\vec v_1\ \vec v_2\ \cdots\ \vec v_n\,],\qquad D=\begin{bmatrix}\lambda_1&&\\&\ddots&\\&&\lambda_n\end{bmatrix}.$$

Intuition · change to the "eigen-coordinates" where $A$ is just scaling

$A=PDP^{-1}$ reads right-to-left as a three-step process: $P^{-1}$ rewrites a vector in the eigenvector coordinate system, $D$ simply scales each coordinate by its eigenvalue, and $P$ translates back. In the right coordinates, the complicated matrix $A$ is nothing but independent stretches. That's why eigenvectors matter: they're the coordinate axes that make $A$ diagonal.

Example 5 · computing $A^k$ painlessly

For $A=\begin{bmatrix}7&2\\-4&1\end{bmatrix}$ you're told $A=PDP^{-1}$ with $P=\begin{bmatrix}1&1\\-1&-2\end{bmatrix}$, $D=\begin{bmatrix}5&0\\0&3\end{bmatrix}$. (Indeed the characteristic equation $\lambda^2-8\lambda+15=0$ gives $\lambda=3,5$.) The magic: in $A^k=(PDP^{-1})^k$, every interior $P^{-1}P$ cancels to $I$, leaving

$$A^k=PD^kP^{-1},\qquad D^k=\begin{bmatrix}5^k&0\\0&3^k\end{bmatrix}.$$

Diagonal powers are trivial (just raise each diagonal entry). With $P^{-1}=\begin{bmatrix}2&1\\-1&-1\end{bmatrix}$, multiplying out gives the closed form

$$A^k=\begin{bmatrix}2\cdot5^k-3^k & 5^k-3^k\\ 2\cdot3^k-2\cdot5^k & 2\cdot3^k-5^k\end{bmatrix}.$$

No repeated matrix multiplication — one formula valid for every $k\ge1$. This is the headline application of diagonalization.

Tip · verify without computing $P^{-1}$

To check $A=PDP^{-1}$ you don't need the inverse: just confirm $AP=PD$ (multiply both sides of $A=PDP^{-1}$ by $P$ on the right). Column by column this is exactly "$A\vec v_i=\lambda_i\vec v_i$", the eigenvector equations — a quick sanity check.

6 · When is a matrix diagonalizable?

Theorem · the diagonalizability criterion

An $n\times n$ matrix $A$ is diagonalizable if and only if $A$ has $n$ linearly independent eigenvectors. In that case, those eigenvectors are the columns of $P$ and the corresponding eigenvalues are the diagonal of $D$.

Example 6 · diagonalizing despite a repeated eigenvalue

Diagonalize $A=\begin{bmatrix}1&3&3\\-3&-5&-3\\3&3&1\end{bmatrix}$.

Step 1 — eigenvalues: $\det(A-\lambda I)=-(\lambda-1)(\lambda+2)^2$, so $\lambda=1$ and $\lambda=-2$ (doubled).

Step 2 — eigenvectors: $\lambda=1$ gives $\vec v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-1\\1\end{bmatrix}$; the repeated $\lambda=-2$ gives two independent ones $\vec v_2=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\1\\0\end{bmatrix},\vec v_3=\begin{bmatrix}-1\\0\\1\end{bmatrix}$. That's $3$ independent eigenvectors in total — enough!

Steps 3–4 — assemble: $P=\begin{bmatrix}1&-1&-1\\-1&1&0\\1&0&1\end{bmatrix}$, $D=\begin{bmatrix}1&0&0\\0&-2&0\\0&0&-2\end{bmatrix}$. Verify with $AP=PD$ (both equal $\begin{bmatrix}1&2&2\\-1&-2&0\\1&0&-2\end{bmatrix}$) — no need to find $P^{-1}$. So $A$ is diagonalizable.

Definition · algebraic vs geometric multiplicity

  • The algebraic multiplicity (AM) of $\lambda$ is how many times it appears as a root of the characteristic polynomial.
  • The geometric multiplicity (GM) of $\lambda$ is the number of independent eigenvectors for $\lambda$ (the number of free variables in $(A-\lambda I)\vec x=\mathbf 0$).

Always $1\le\text{GM}\le\text{AM}$. The eigenvalue "demands" AM eigenvectors but only "supplies" GM of them.

Theorem · the clean diagonalizability test

$A$ is diagonalizable $\iff$ for every eigenvalue, $\text{GM}=\text{AM}$. (Equivalently, the independent eigenvectors total $n$.) Since GM=AM automatically when AM=1, you only need to check the repeated eigenvalues.

Trap · a repeated eigenvalue can starve you of eigenvectors

Compare Example 4's matrices $B$ and $C$: both had a doubled eigenvalue. $B$ supplied two independent eigenvectors for it (GM=AM=2 → diagonalizable). $C$ supplied only one (GM=1 < AM=2 → not diagonalizable, "defective"). The lesson: a repeated eigenvalue is a red flag — you must actually count its eigenvectors. It's only when GM falls short of AM that diagonalization fails.

A sufficient (not necessary) shortcut

Theorem: eigenvectors for distinct eigenvalues are automatically linearly independent. Corollary: if an $n\times n$ matrix has $n$ distinct eigenvalues, it has $n$ independent eigenvectors and is guaranteed diagonalizable (as in Example 4's matrix $A$, with eigenvalues $1,2,3$). Caution: this is sufficient but not necessary — a matrix with repeated eigenvalues can still be diagonalizable (matrix $B$), as long as each repeated eigenvalue supplies enough eigenvectors.

Why distinct eigenvalues force independence (idea)

Suppose eigenvectors $\vec x_1,\dots,\vec x_k$ for distinct $\lambda_1,\dots,\lambda_k$ satisfied a dependence $c_1\vec x_1+\cdots+c_k\vec x_k=\mathbf 0$. Applying $A$ multiplies each term by its $\lambda_i$; subtracting a suitable multiple of the original relation cancels one vector and leaves a shorter relation whose coefficients carry factors $(\lambda_i-\lambda_j)\ne0$. Induction forces all $c_i=0$. So no nontrivial dependence exists — the eigenvectors are independent. $\blacksquare$

7 · Section recap & the big picture

The §3.5 playbook

  • Eigen-equation: $A\vec x=\lambda\vec x$, $\vec x\ne\mathbf 0$. $\vec x$ = eigenvector (a direction $A$ only scales), $\lambda$ = eigenvalue (the scale factor).
  • Find $\lambda$: solve the characteristic equation $\det(A-\lambda I)=0$. Find $\vec x$: for each $\lambda$, solve $(A-\lambda I)\vec x=\mathbf 0$ (report whole-number representatives; multiples are the same eigenvector).
  • Triangular/diagonal → eigenvalues are the diagonal entries, free.
  • Diagonalize if you have $n$ independent eigenvectors: $A=PDP^{-1}$ with eigenvectors as columns of $P$, eigenvalues on $D$. Then $A^k=PD^kP^{-1}$ makes powers trivial. Check via $AP=PD$.
  • Diagonalizable $\iff$ GM = AM for every eigenvalue (only repeated eigenvalues need checking). $n$ distinct eigenvalues guarantee it; a repeated eigenvalue short on eigenvectors (GM < AM) breaks it.

How §3.5 ties the whole chapter together

Eigenvalues sit at the crossroads of everything you've learned: finding them needs determinants (§3.3); finding eigenvectors is solving a homogeneous system (§3.1); counting independent eigenvectors is a linear-independence question (§3.2); and $\lambda=0$ being an eigenvalue is exactly the statement that $A$ is singular (§3.3). Master eigenvalues and you've genuinely understood what a matrix does — not just how to compute with it.
