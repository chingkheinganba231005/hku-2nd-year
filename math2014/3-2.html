SECTION 3.2

Vectors, Linear Combinations, Span & Independence

In §3.1 a column of numbers was just "the answer, stacked up." In §3.2 those columns become the main characters. We stop asking "what solves this system?" and start asking richer questions: can I build this vector out of those vectors? which directions can I reach? is one of my vectors secretly redundant? Remarkably, every one of these questions turns back into "solve a linear system", so you already own the tool. This section is where linear algebra stops being bookkeeping and starts being geometry.

1 · Vectors in $\R^2$: lists you can add and stretch

A column vector (or just vector) is a matrix with a single column, an ordered list of numbers written vertically. The simplest live in $\R^2$ ("r-two"), with two entries each.

Definition · $\R^2$ and the two operations

A vector in $\R^2$ is $\displaystyle \vec w=\begin{bmatrix}w_1\\w_2\end{bmatrix}$ with $w_1,w_2$ real numbers. The symbol $\R^2$ means the set of all such two-entry vectors. Two vectors are equal exactly when their entries match position by position. There are two operations:

  • Addition: add entrywise. $\begin{bmatrix}u_1\\u_2\end{bmatrix}+\begin{bmatrix}v_1\\v_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}u_1+v_1\\u_2+v_2\end{bmatrix}$.
  • Scalar multiple: multiply every entry by a number $c$ (a scalar). $c\begin{bmatrix}u_1\\u_2\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}cu_1\\cu_2\end{bmatrix}$.

Intuition · a vector is an arrow; the two operations are "slide" and "stretch"

Picture $\vec w$ as an arrow from the origin to the point $(w_1,w_2)$. Then scalar multiplication stretches or flips the arrow: $2\vec w$ is twice as long, same direction; $-\vec w$ is the same length pointing backwards; $0\vec w$ collapses to the origin. Addition slides one arrow onto the tip of the other. These two moves, stretch and slide, are the only things we ever do to vectors, and the whole section is about what you can build with them.

Example 1 · scaling and combining two vectors

Given $\vec u=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\end{bmatrix}$ and $\vec v=\begin{bmatrix}2\\-5\end{bmatrix}$, find $4\vec u$, $(-3)\vec v$, and $4\vec u+(-3)\vec v$.

Scale each entry, then add entrywise:

$$4\vec u=\begin{bmatrix}4\\-8\end{bmatrix},\qquad -3\vec v=\begin{bmatrix}-6\\15\end{bmatrix},\qquad 4\vec u-3\vec v=\begin{bmatrix}4+(-6)\\-8+15\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}-2\\7\end{bmatrix}.$$

That's it, scale first, then add position by position. No new rules, just the two operations applied carefully.

2 · Vectors are points: the geometric picture

Set up ordinary $xy$-axes. Every point $(a,b)$ in the plane corresponds to exactly one vector $\begin{bmatrix}a\\b\end{bmatrix}$, and vice versa. So we are free to think of $\R^2$ either as "all two-entry columns" or as "all points in the plane", whichever is more helpful. This dictionary between algebra (columns of numbers) and geometry (points and arrows) is the heart of the subject.

Intuition · the parallelogram rule for addition

Draw $\vec u$ and $\vec v$ as arrows from the origin $\mathbf 0$. Then $\vec u+\vec v$ is the arrow to the fourth corner of the parallelogram whose other three corners are $\vec u$, $\mathbf 0$, and $\vec v$. In words: addition "completes the parallelogram". This picture is why vector addition feels like combining two movements, go along $\vec u$, then along $\vec v$, and you land at $\vec u+\vec v$, no matter which you do first (that's why $\vec u+\vec v=\vec v+\vec u$).

3 · Up to $\R^3$ and $\R^n$: more entries, same rules

Nothing special happened because we had two entries. Everything generalises directly.

Definition · $\R^n$

Vectors in $\R^3$ are $3\times1$ columns (three entries), pictured as arrows from the origin in 3-D space. For any positive integer $n$, $\R^n$ ("r-n") is the set of all ordered $n$-tuples of real numbers, written as $n\times1$ columns $\vec u=\begin{bmatrix}u_1\\u_2\\\vdots\\u_n\end{bmatrix}$. Addition is still entrywise, scalar multiplication still scales every entry. The zero vector $\mathbf 0$ has every entry $0$.

Tip · you can't picture $\R^5$, and you don't need to

Beyond $\R^3$ there's no literal picture, but all the algebra is identical and the 2-D/3-D intuition keeps working as a metaphor. When a problem lives in $\R^6$, reason as if the arrows were in the plane, "stretch", "slide", "same direction", the conclusions carry over because they only ever used the two operations, never the drawing.

Algebraic properties of $\R^n$ (the vector-space laws)

For all $\vec u,\vec v,\vec w$ in $\R^n$ and all scalars $c,d$:

(i)$\vec u+\vec v=\vec v+\vec u$(addition order doesn't matter)
(ii)$(\vec u+\vec v)+\vec w=\vec u+(\vec v+\vec w)$(grouping doesn't matter)
(iii)$\vec u+\mathbf 0=\mathbf 0+\vec u=\vec u$($\mathbf 0$ changes nothing)
(iv)$\vec u+(-\vec u)=\mathbf 0$, where $-\vec u=(-1)\vec u$(every vector has an opposite)
(v)$c(\vec u+\vec v)=c\vec u+c\vec v$(scaling distributes over vectors)
(vi)$(c+d)\vec u=c\vec u+d\vec u$(scaling distributes over scalars)

Intuition · these six laws are just "common sense, made official"

You already expect all six, that's the point. They say vectors behave like ordinary numbers for the purposes of adding and scaling. They matter because every later theorem is built only from these laws, so anything that obeys them (not just columns, functions and polynomials too) is a "vector space" and inherits the whole theory for free. For this course, just use them without fuss when rearranging vector equations.

4 · Linear combinations: the one idea everything rests on

Here is the central construction of the whole chapter. Take some vectors, scale each by a number, add the results. That's a linear combination, the only way we're allowed to build new vectors from old ones.

Definition · Linear combination

Given vectors $\vec u_1,\vec u_2,\dots,\vec u_n$ (all the same size) and scalars $\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\dots,\alpha_n$ (the weights), the vector

$$\alpha_1\vec u_1+\alpha_2\vec u_2+\cdots+\alpha_n\vec u_n$$

is called a linear combination of $\vec u_1,\dots,\vec u_n$ with those weights. The weights can be any numbers, positive, negative, zero, fractions.

Example 2 · same vectors, different weights, different results

Let $\vec u_1=\begin{bmatrix}2\\4\\-3\\1\\2\\9\end{bmatrix},\ \vec u_2=\begin{bmatrix}6\\3\\0\\-2\\1\\4\end{bmatrix},\ \vec u_3=\begin{bmatrix}-5\\2\\1\\1\\-3\\0\end{bmatrix},\ \vec u_4=\begin{bmatrix}3\\2\\-5\\7\\1\\3\end{bmatrix}$ (vectors in $\R^6$).

(1) With weights $\alpha_1=1,\ \alpha_2=-4,\ \alpha_3=2,\ \alpha_4=-1$, compute $\alpha_1\vec u_1+\alpha_2\vec u_2+\alpha_3\vec u_3+\alpha_4\vec u_4$. Work one entry at a time, e.g. the first entry is $1(2)+(-4)(6)+2(-5)+(-1)(3)=2-24-10-3=-35$. Doing all six entries:

$$1\vec u_1-4\vec u_2+2\vec u_3-\vec u_4=\begin{bmatrix}-35\\-6\\4\\4\\-9\\-10\end{bmatrix}.$$

(2) Keep the same four vectors but take new weights $\beta_1=3,\ \beta_2=0,\ \beta_3=5,\ \beta_4=-1$. (Weight $0$ means $\vec u_2$ simply doesn't contribute.) Then

$$3\vec u_1+0\vec u_2+5\vec u_3-\vec u_4=\begin{bmatrix}-22\\20\\1\\1\\-10\\24\end{bmatrix}.$$

The lesson: fix a set of vectors, and by turning the weight-knobs you manufacture a whole universe of new vectors. Which vectors you can reach this way is precisely the idea of span (part 6).

Tip · compute linear combinations entry-by-entry, with a column of scratch

For a 6-entry combination, don't try to do it in your head. Write the weight times each vector's first entry in a row and sum; then the second entries; and so on. It's slow but bullet-proof. A single sign slip ruins the whole vector, and these appear constantly, so trade a little speed for accuracy.

5 · The key question: is $\vec b$ a linear combination of given vectors?

Example 2 built vectors by choosing weights. Now reverse it: you're handed a target vector $\vec b$ and asked whether some choice of weights produces it. This "can I build $\vec b$?" question is the bridge back to §3.1.

Example 3 · can $\vec b$ be written as a combination of $\vec a_1,\vec a_2$?

Let $\vec a_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\\-5\end{bmatrix},\ \vec a_2=\begin{bmatrix}2\\5\\6\end{bmatrix},\ \vec b=\begin{bmatrix}7\\4\\-3\end{bmatrix}$. Do weights $x_1,x_2$ exist with $x_1\vec a_1+x_2\vec a_2=\vec b$? If so, find them.

Turn the vector equation into a system. The left side is

$$x_1\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\\-5\end{bmatrix}+x_2\begin{bmatrix}2\\5\\6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}x_1+2x_2\\-2x_1+5x_2\\-5x_1+6x_2\end{bmatrix}.$$

Setting this equal to $\vec b$ entry-by-entry gives three ordinary equations:

$$x_1+2x_2=7,\qquad -2x_1+5x_2=4,\qquad -5x_1+6x_2=-3.$$

Solve by row reduction (exactly §3.1). The system is consistent with the unique solution $x_1=3,\ x_2=2$. So yes: $\vec b=3\vec a_1+2\vec a_2$. (Quick check: $3\begin{bmatrix}1\\-2\\-5\end{bmatrix}+2\begin{bmatrix}2\\5\\6\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}3+4\\-6+10\\-15+12\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}7\\4\\-3\end{bmatrix}=\vec b$. ✓)

Intuition · the vectors you combine are literally the columns of the matrix

Look where the numbers landed: $\vec a_1$, $\vec a_2$, and $\vec b$ became the columns of the augmented matrix $\left[\begin{array}{cc|c}1&2&7\\-2&5&4\\-5&6&-3\end{array}\right]=[\,\vec a_1\ \vec a_2\mid\vec b\,]$. That's not a coincidence, it's the whole trick.

The fundamental correspondence

The vector equation $\;x_1\vec a_1+x_2\vec a_2+\cdots+x_n\vec a_n=\vec b\;$ has exactly the same solution set as the linear system whose augmented matrix is

$$[\,\vec a_1\ \ \vec a_2\ \cdots\ \vec a_n\mid\vec b\,].$$

In particular: $\vec b$ is a linear combination of $\vec a_1,\dots,\vec a_n$ if and only if that system is consistent (has at least one solution).

Tip · "linear combination?" is a consistency question in disguise

Whenever a problem asks "is $\vec b$ a combination of these vectors?", your move is automatic: stack the vectors as columns, tack $\vec b$ on as the last column, row-reduce, check consistency. Consistent → yes (and the solution gives the weights); a $0=\text{nonzero}$ row → no. You never need a new technique; §3.1 already answers every such question.

6 · Span: the set of everything you can reach

Fix some vectors. As you let the weights range over all numbers, the linear combinations sweep out a set of reachable vectors. That set has a name.

Definition · Span

If $\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_p$ are in $\R^n$, then $\operatorname{Span}\{\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_p\}$ is the set of all linear combinations $c_1\vec v_1+c_2\vec v_2+\cdots+c_p\vec v_p$ as the scalars $c_1,\dots,c_p$ range over all real numbers. We say this set is spanned (or generated) by $\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_p$.

Intuition · span = "all the places these arrows can take you"

Think of the $\vec v_i$ as the only moves you're allowed (in any amount, forward or backward). The span is every destination reachable by combining those moves. Asking "is $\vec b$ in $\operatorname{Span}\{\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_p\}$?" is the same question as "is $\vec b$ a linear combination of the $\vec v_i$?", which (part 5) is just "is $[\,\vec v_1\ \cdots\ \vec v_p\mid\vec b\,]$ consistent?". Three phrasings, one computation.

Tip · the geometry of small spans (memorise these pictures)

  • $\operatorname{Span}\{\vec v\}$ for one nonzero $\vec v$: all scalar multiples of $\vec v$, a line through the origin in the direction of $\vec v$.
  • $\operatorname{Span}\{\vec u,\vec v\}$ for two nonzero vectors, neither a multiple of the other: the whole plane through the origin containing both arrows.
  • If $\vec v$ is a multiple of $\vec u$: they point along the same line, so $\operatorname{Span}\{\vec u,\vec v\}$ collapses back to just that line, the second vector added nothing.

Every span contains $\mathbf 0$ (take all weights $0$) and is "flat" through the origin, a point, line, plane, or higher-dimensional flat. That last bullet is your first hint that some vectors can be redundant, which is exactly linear dependence.

7 · Linear independence: are any of my vectors redundant?

If one vector in a set is already reachable from the others, it adds no new directions, it's dead weight. "Linear independence" is the precise way to say "no dead weight." The cleanest test uses the zero vector.

Definition · Linearly independent / dependent

A set $\{\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_p\}$ in $\R^n$ is linearly independent if the vector equation

$$x_1\vec v_1+x_2\vec v_2+\cdots+x_p\vec v_p=\mathbf 0$$

has only the trivial solution $x_1=\cdots=x_p=0$. The set is linearly dependent if there exist weights $c_1,\dots,c_p$, not all zero, with $c_1\vec v_1+\cdots+c_p\vec v_p=\mathbf 0$. Such an equation (with not-all-zero weights) is a linear dependence relation. A set is dependent exactly when it is not independent.

Intuition · "the only way to get zero is to use nothing"

There's always one boring way to combine vectors into $\mathbf 0$: multiply them all by $0$. The question is whether that's the only way. If some nonzero mix also cancels to $\mathbf 0$, then you can solve that relation for one of the vectors, showing it's a combination of the others, redundant. So:

  • Independent = the only way to reach $\mathbf 0$ is the trivial way = no vector is redundant.
  • Dependent = there's a sneaky nonzero cancellation = at least one vector is a combination of the rest.

Matrix form · independence $\Leftrightarrow$ only the trivial solution of $A\vec x=\mathbf 0$

Put the vectors as columns: $A=[\,\vec a_1\ \cdots\ \vec a_n\,]$. Then $x_1\vec a_1+\cdots+x_n\vec a_n=\mathbf 0$ is exactly the homogeneous system $A\vec x=\mathbf 0$. Therefore:

The columns of $A$ are linearly independent $\iff$ $A\vec x=\mathbf 0$ has only the trivial solution $\iff$ there are no free variables.

So testing independence is just reducing the coefficient matrix and counting free variables, zero free variables means independent; any free variable means dependent.

Example 4 · test three vectors, and exhibit a dependence relation

Let $\vec v_1=\begin{bmatrix}1\\2\\3\end{bmatrix},\ \vec v_2=\begin{bmatrix}4\\5\\6\end{bmatrix},\ \vec v_3=\begin{bmatrix}2\\1\\0\end{bmatrix}$.

(a) Independent? Set $\alpha_1\vec v_1+\alpha_2\vec v_2+\alpha_3\vec v_3=\mathbf 0$, which is the homogeneous system

$$\alpha_1+4\alpha_2+2\alpha_3=0,\qquad 2\alpha_1+5\alpha_2+\alpha_3=0,\qquad 3\alpha_1+6\alpha_2=0.$$

Row-reduce the coefficient matrix. It reduces to the two relations $\alpha_1+2\alpha_3=0$ and $\alpha_2+\alpha_3=0$, with $\alpha_3$ free, so there are nontrivial solutions. For instance $\alpha_3=-1$ gives $\alpha_1=2,\ \alpha_2=1$, i.e. $(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3)=(-2,1,-1)$ also works (scaling the free choice). Because a nonzero solution exists, the set is linearly dependent (not independent).

(b) A dependence relation. Using $(\alpha_1,\alpha_2,\alpha_3)=(-2,1,-1)$: $\;-2\vec v_1+\vec v_2-\vec v_3=\mathbf 0$, which rearranges to the tidy fact

$$2\vec v_1=\vec v_2-\vec v_3.$$

Check: $\vec v_2-\vec v_3=\begin{bmatrix}4-2\\5-1\\6-0\end{bmatrix}=\begin{bmatrix}2\\4\\6\end{bmatrix}=2\vec v_1$. ✓ So $\vec v_1$ really is built from the other two, that's the redundancy made explicit.

8 · Fast rules for small or special sets

Some cases you should recognise instantly without any row reduction.

One or two vectors

  • One vector $\{\vec v\}$: independent $\iff$ $\vec v\ne\mathbf 0$. (If $\vec v\ne\mathbf 0$ then $x_1\vec v=\mathbf 0$ forces $x_1=0$.)
  • The zero vector alone $\{\mathbf 0\}$: dependent, since $x_1\mathbf 0=\mathbf 0$ holds for every $x_1$, including nonzero ones.
  • Two vectors $\{\vec v_1,\vec v_2\}$: dependent $\iff$ one is a scalar multiple of the other. Independent $\iff$ neither is a multiple of the other. (For two vectors you can often just eyeball this.)

Theorem* · characterization of dependent sets

A set $S=\{\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_p\}$ of two or more vectors is linearly dependent if and only if at least one vector in $S$ is a linear combination of the others. Moreover, if $S$ is dependent and $\vec v_1\ne\mathbf 0$, then some $\vec v_j$ (with $j>1$) is a combination of the preceding vectors $\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_{j-1}$.

Why Theorem* is true (both directions, in plain words)

If one vector is a combination of the others, say $\vec v_1=c_2\vec v_2+c_3\vec v_3$, move everything to one side: $\;(-1)\vec v_1+c_2\vec v_2+c_3\vec v_3+0\vec v_4+\cdots=\mathbf 0$. The weight $-1$ on $\vec v_1$ is nonzero, so that's a genuine dependence relation, $S$ is dependent.

Conversely, if $S$ is dependent, there's a relation $c_1\vec v_1+\cdots+c_p\vec v_p=\mathbf 0$ with not all $c_i$ zero. Let $j$ be the largest index with $c_j\ne 0$. If $j=1$ the relation says $c_1\vec v_1=\mathbf 0$ with $c_1\ne0$, forcing $\vec v_1=\mathbf 0$ (handled as the trivial case). Otherwise $j>1$, and we can divide by $c_j$ and solve:

$$\vec v_j=\left(-\frac{c_1}{c_j}\right)\vec v_1+\cdots+\left(-\frac{c_{j-1}}{c_j}\right)\vec v_{j-1},$$

so $\vec v_j$ is a combination of the vectors before it. $\blacksquare$

Trap · "dependent" does not mean "every vector is redundant"

Theorem* promises at least one vector is a combination of the others, not all of them. A particular vector in a dependent set may still fail to be a combination of the rest (e.g. if it points in a brand-new direction while two other vectors duplicate each other). Don't over-claim: dependence is a statement about the set, not a licence to delete any vector you like.

Example 5 · when is $\vec w$ in $\operatorname{Span}\{\vec u,\vec v\}$?

Let $\vec u=\begin{bmatrix}3\\1\\0\end{bmatrix},\ \vec v=\begin{bmatrix}1\\6\\0\end{bmatrix}$. Describe $\operatorname{Span}\{\vec u,\vec v\}$, and explain why a vector $\vec w$ lies in it exactly when $\{\vec u,\vec v,\vec w\}$ is linearly dependent.

Neither $\vec u$ nor $\vec v$ is a multiple of the other, so they're independent and span a plane through the origin, here the $x_1x_2$-plane (both have third entry $0$, so every combination does too). Now:

  • If $\vec w$ is in the plane ($\vec w=c_1\vec u+c_2\vec v$ for some weights), then $\vec w$ is a combination of $\vec u,\vec v$, so by Theorem* the set $\{\vec u,\vec v,\vec w\}$ is dependent.
  • Conversely, if $\{\vec u,\vec v,\vec w\}$ is dependent, Theorem* (with $\vec u\ne\mathbf 0$) says some vector is a combination of the preceding ones. It can't be $\vec v$ (not a multiple of $\vec u$), so it must be $\vec w$, i.e. $\vec w$ is a combination of $\vec u,\vec v$, so $\vec w$ is in the plane.

So "$\vec w\in\operatorname{Span}\{\vec u,\vec v\}$" and "$\{\vec u,\vec v,\vec w\}$ dependent" are the same condition: geometrically, $\vec w$ lies in the plane spanned by $\vec u$ and $\vec v$. If $\vec w$ pokes out of the plane, the three are independent.

9 · Two theorems that let you conclude dependence instantly

Sometimes you can declare a set dependent without any computation at all, just by counting or spotting a zero. Keep these two in your back pocket for exams.

Theorem** · too many vectors must be dependent

If a set contains more vectors than there are entries in each vector, the set is automatically linearly dependent. That is, any set $\{\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_p\}$ in $\R^n$ with $p>n$ is linearly dependent.

Why Theorem** holds · more unknowns than equations

Form $A=[\,\vec v_1\ \cdots\ \vec v_p\,]$, which is $n\times p$. The equation $A\vec x=\mathbf 0$ is a homogeneous system of $n$ equations in $p$ unknowns. If $p>n$ there are more variables than equations, so there must be a free variable, hence a nontrivial solution, hence dependence. Example: any 3 vectors in $\R^2$, or any 5 vectors in $\R^4$, are dependent before you even look at the numbers. (Careful: the theorem says nothing when $p\le n$, then you must actually test.)

Theorem*** · a set containing $\mathbf 0$ is dependent

If a set $S=\{\vec v_1,\dots,\vec v_p\}$ in $\R^n$ contains the zero vector, it is linearly dependent.

Why Theorem*** holds · put all the weight on the zero vector

Say $\vec v_1=\mathbf 0$. Then $1\vec v_1+0\vec v_2+\cdots+0\vec v_p=\mathbf 0$ is a dependence relation with a nonzero weight (the $1$ on $\vec v_1$) that costs nothing, because $1\cdot\mathbf 0=\mathbf 0$. A nonzero weight exists, so the set is dependent. Spot a zero vector → declare dependent immediately.

10 · Section recap & exam tactics

The §3.2 playbook

  • Everything is one system. "Is $\vec b$ a combination / in the span?" → reduce $[\,\vec v_1\cdots\vec v_p\mid\vec b\,]$ and check consistency. "Are they independent?" → reduce the coefficient matrix $[\,\vec v_1\cdots\vec v_p\,]$ and count free variables (the constant column is all zeros, so you can omit it).
  • Consistency vs free variables are different questions. Combination/span cares whether a solution exists; independence cares whether the solution is unique. Don't mix them up.
  • Use the free shortcuts first: more vectors than entries → dependent (Theorem**); a zero vector present → dependent (Theorem***); two vectors with one a multiple of the other → dependent.
  • To exhibit a dependence relation, solve the homogeneous system, pick a convenient nonzero value for a free variable, and write out $c_1\vec v_1+\cdots=\mathbf 0$. Then rearrange to show one vector built from the others, and verify by direct computation.
  • Geometry anchors the algebra: one vector spans a line, two independent vectors span a plane, a vector is in a span exactly when it lies in that line/plane. Picture it to sanity-check your arithmetic.
